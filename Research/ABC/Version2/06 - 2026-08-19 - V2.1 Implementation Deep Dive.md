## 1. What this is, in one paragraph

vLLM can spill KV cache to CPU DRAM and from there to NVMe. Today that movement is **reactive**: a block is fetched back only when a request's prefix lookup demands it, which puts the transfer squarely on the critical path for time-to-first-token. V2.1 adds a **proactive** path: when a request is admitted to the scheduler queue but has not yet run, the engine already knows every KV block that request will need. If those blocks are sitting on NVMe, and if the request is going to wait in the queue longer than the transfer takes, we can move them to CPU *while it waits* and the transfer costs nothing. The whole design is about making "if" precise enough to be safe.

---

## 2. Why: what V1 established and where it stopped

Version 1 built the mechanism — trigger prefetch at admission, submit NVMe→CPU promotions for the first *N* chunks of the request — and proved it executes. What it could not do is choose *well*. It assumed the blocks were on NVMe rather than checking, and it had no notion of whether there was time to move them.

The two AgentX/Weka runs are the evidence that shaped V2:

Read Figure 1 as three separate failures, because they have three separate causes:

- **Load-failed 87% → 38%.** V1 never asked whether a key was on NVMe; it assumed. Most of the time it was wrong. This is a *residency* problem and no amount of queue pressure fixes it.
- **Late 98.5% → 42%.** At C32 the queue was essentially empty (mean waiting < 0.25), so there was no window to hide a transfer in. This is a *lead time* problem, and it is why the C64 numbers are so much better.
- **Redundant 91% → 73%.** The first *N* chunks of a request are usually the shared conversational prefix, which is already in CPU. This is a *selection* problem: V1 was looking in the wrong place.

V2.1 addresses residency and lead time directly, and addresses selection by starting where the request's cached prefix ends rather than at chunk zero.

## 3. The core idea

Promote a bundle **B** of KV chunks only when all three hold:

1. **B is usable.** It is an ordered, contiguous run of chunks starting exactly where the request's CPU-resident prefix ends.
2. **B is there.** Every chunk in B has been *confirmed* resident on the secondary tier, not assumed.
3. **B fits in the gap.** The predicted remaining lead time exceeds the measured time to move B.

$$\text{promote}(B) \iff H_{\text{remaining}} > L_{\text{prefetch}}(B)$$

Everything below is the machinery that makes each of those three conditions mean something concrete.

---

## 4. Condition 1: why bundles must be ordered and contiguous

vLLM block hashes are **prefix-chained**. The hash of chunk *k* is computed over the hash of chunk *k−1* plus its own tokens:

$$K_k = H(K_{k-1},\ \text{tokens}_k)$$

Two consequences follow, and together they fully determine the shape of the selection unit.

First, the demand-side scan stops at the first miss. In `_maximal_prefix_lookup` (`offloading/scheduler.py`), a `MISS` breaks the loop:

```python
case LookupResult.MISS:
    break
```

So if chunk 5 is absent, chunks 6 and 7 can never be reached by the demand path *even if they are present*. Prefetching them is guaranteed-wasted work.

Second, the storage tier is append-like, so a stored chunk *k* normally implies chunk *k−1* was stored too. This is precisely why the original V1 design (read ahead *after* a demand miss) was rejected: it selected candidates starting at the one point where storage had just demonstrated the continuation does not exist.

The selection unit is therefore a **prefix bundle**: an ordered, contiguous slice beginning at the primary-residency frontier.

```
request keys:  [ k0  k1  k2 | k3  k4  k5 | k6  k7  ... ]
                 already in    candidate     dropped:
                 CPU  (skip)   bundle B      past the
                               (promote)     first absent key
                 ^frontier m               ^first ABSENT
```

The frontier scan walks the key list once against the CPU tier and stops at the first key that is neither `HIT` nor `HIT_PENDING`. That index *m* is where the bundle starts. This alone addresses V1's redundancy problem: V1 kept re-selecting the hot shared prefix, and the frontier scan skips exactly that.

## 5. Condition 2: residency is asynchronous, so it needs a state machine

The obvious implementation — ask the tier "is this key present?" at admission and act on the answer — does not work, and the reason is worth understanding because it shaped the whole control flow.

The filesystem tier resolves lookups on a **worker thread**. `AsyncLookupManager.lookup()` returns a tri-state:

```python
True   # present
False  # absent
None   # not resolved yet, retry next step
```

Pending lookups accumulate into a batch that is posted to the worker in `flush()`, which the tier calls from its `on_schedule_end`. Results are drained no earlier than the *next* step. So a lookup issued during step *k* resolves at step *k+1* at the earliest — there is a **minimum one-step latency** and no single callback can consume the answer.

(Guide [[03 - Event-Driven Temperature Heuristic Implementation Guide|03]] §3.7 claimed results were consumable in the same step. That is wrong; it was corrected here and confirmed empirically — the fs-tier end-to-end test observes the bundle sitting in `PENDING_LOOKUP` for two steps before resolving.)

The policy therefore owns an explicit per-bundle state machine, re-driven every scheduler step:

```
                    ┌─────────────────┐
   admission ─────► │ PENDING_LOOKUP  │ ◄──┐ re-driven each step
                    └────────┬────────┘    │ until resolved
                             │             │ (RETRY loops here)
              ┌──────────────┼─────────────┘
              ▼              ▼
        ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐
        │ RESIDENT │   │  ABSENT  │  (terminal)
        └────┬─────┘   └──────────┘
             ▼
        ┌──────────┐   deadline / utility
        │   GATE   ├──► GATE_REJECTED, LATE, CAPACITY_SKIPPED (terminal)
        └────┬─────┘
             ▼
        ┌───────────┐
        │ SUBMITTED ├──► READY | LATE | FAILED   (terminal)
        └───────────┘
```

The rules that make this safe:

- **Pending is never absence.** A `RETRY` keeps the bundle in `PENDING_LOOKUP`. Counting it as absent would systematically under-report residency.
- **Deadlines keep ticking while pending.** Lookup delay consumes lead time, so a bundle whose residency resolves late may correctly fail a deadline it would have passed at admission.
- **Cancellation on finish and preemption.** When a request finishes, its non-submitted bundle is cancelled and dropped from the active set *immediately* — before the tier's own `AsyncLookupManager.cleanup()` runs. This ordering matters: if the policy issued one more lookup after cleanup, it would repopulate the tier's reverse index and leak that entry permanently.
- **Submitted bundles are left alone.** A promotion already in flight completes normally even if its request finishes; `complete_store` touches no per-request state, so this is safe.

## 6. Condition 3: the deadline gate, and where lead time comes from

### Estimating lead time

The policy has no oracle for "when will this request first run". It estimates:

$$H = \text{queue\_position} \times \overline{\Delta t}_{\text{schedule}}$$

where `queue_position` is the number of requests admitted-but-not-yet-first-scheduled ahead of this one, and $\overline{\Delta t}_{\text{schedule}}$ is an EWMA of the interval between first-schedule events, divided by batch size so a batched dequeue does not register as a burst of zero-length intervals:

```python
def on_first_scheduled(self, now, batch_size, queue_remains_nonempty):
    if batch_size <= 0:
        return
    if self._last_scheduled_at is not None and now > self._last_scheduled_at:
        interval_ms = (now - self._last_scheduled_at) * 1000.0 / batch_size
        self._ewma_interval_ms += self._alpha * (interval_ms - self._ewma_interval_ms)
    self._last_scheduled_at = now if queue_remains_nonempty else None
```

This is Little's law in disguise. For a queue with mean length $L$ and throughput $\lambda$, mean wait is $W = L/\lambda$; here $\overline{\Delta t}_{\text{schedule}} \approx 1/\lambda$ and `queue_position` is this request's share of $L$.

A consequence worth stating plainly, because it is the mechanism V1 measured: **a request admitted to an empty queue predicts H = 0 and is correctly never prefetched.** That is the C32 regime. Prefetch is not free work you do when idle; it is work you hide inside a wait that already exists.

One subtlety that took a bug to find. The per-request opt-in `kv_transfer_params.abc_admission_prefetch` gates whether the policy hears about a request at all. Early on, queue position was counted only over opted-in requests, so a marked request behind fifty unmarked ones predicted H = 0. The fix routes **every** admission through `on_request_enqueued()` (called unconditionally from the manager's `on_new_request`) while bundles are still created only for opted-in requests.

### Estimating transfer cost — measured, not configured

The gate compares H against the time to move the bundle:

$$L_{\text{prefetch}}(B) = \text{base} + \text{per\_chunk} \cdot |B|$$

Both constants are **measured from real transfers**, not configured. Every completed secondary→primary promotion contributes a $(n, y)$ sample — batch size and elapsed milliseconds — to a decayed weighted least-squares fit:

$$
\text{slope} = \frac{\widehat{\operatorname{cov}}(n, y)}{\widehat{\operatorname{var}}(n)}, \qquad
\text{intercept} = \bar{y} - \text{slope}\cdot\bar{n}
$$

with decayed sufficient statistics $S_w, S_n, S_{nn}, S_y, S_{ny}$ updated in O(1) per sample and multiplied by $d = 0.99$ each time, so the fit tracks drift.

Two properties make this the right shape rather than just a tidier one:

**It calibrates in shadow mode.** The samples come from *demand-driven* promotions, which happen in any offload deployment regardless of whether prefetch is enabled. So the cost model is fully calibrated before a single speculative byte moves.

**Contention becomes self-regulating.** When the tier is busy, observed transfers lengthen, the fitted `per_chunk` rises, $L_{\text{prefetch}}$ grows, and the deadline gate tightens on its own. This is why V2.1 has **no separate utility term**: with a measured cost, the deadline gate *is* the contention control.

That last point is a deliberate departure from the reviewed design, which specified

$$U(B) = p_{\text{use}}\cdot\text{saved\_ms} - \Delta Q_{\text{active}} - \mathbb{E}[C_{\text{evict}}] - C_{\text{fail}}$$

With any sane constants this reduces to $0.9 \times 2 \times |B| > 0$ — always true. It was four configuration knobs producing a foregone conclusion, and $p_{\text{use}}$ and $\text{demand\_load\_per\_chunk}$ only ever appeared as a product, so no experiment could separate them. They were removed. The concept returns when there is a contention cost worth measuring.

The fit refuses to report a result until it can: at least 20 effective samples **and** non-trivial variance in batch size, because slope and intercept are not separately identifiable from batches that are all the same size. Until then it falls back to seeds.

### Seeds derived from the KB, not invented

The seeds were derived from the existing Nemotron measurements rather than guessed:

|---|---|---|

| Chunk size | 16 tokens × 128 KiB | 2 MiB |
| NVMe read (active mean) | Revision 2 device series | 921 MiB/s |

## 7. Non-evicting speculative allocation

A prefetch that evicts a block the running workload is about to need is worse than no prefetch. The reviewed design called for a two-phase `try_reserve_no_evict()` followed by a consuming `_initiate_promotion(..., reserved=…)`.

Reading the code showed that was unnecessary and actively risky. The existing promotion path *already* allocates exactly once, via `prepare_write` → `CPUOffloadingManager.prepare_store`, and the allocated block is created at `ref_cnt = -1` — in-flight, and already excluded from the evictable set. The only real problem was that `prepare_store` may **evict LRU victims** to make room. So the fix is one flag, not a second allocation path:

```python
def prepare_store(self, keys, req_context, *, allow_eviction: bool = True):
    ...
    if num_blocks_to_evict > 0 and not allow_eviction:
        return None
```

Threaded through as `_initiate_promotion(..., allow_eviction=False)` on the speculative path only. Keyword-only with a default, so every existing caller is untouched. Implementing the two-phase design as written would have introduced the double-allocation bug it was meant to prevent.

A second mechanism, added during review, handles the other half of the problem. Blocks that *were* populated speculatively and have not yet been demanded are tracked in a `_speculative` set and reclaimed **before** the cache policy is asked to evict demand data:

```python
speculative = self._speculative_eviction_candidates(num_blocks_to_evict, protected)
normal_count = num_blocks_to_evict - len(speculative)
normal_evicted = self._policy.evict(normal_count, protected | set(self._speculative))
```

Any demand touch — `lookup` hit, `prepare_load`, `touch` — removes a key from `_speculative`, so a block is only ever reclaimed while it is still purely speculative. Net effect: speculative work never displaces demand data, and when memory is tight the speculative work is what gives way.

## 8. Accounting: a partition, enforced structurally

The V1 reports repeatedly ran into denominator ambiguity — adding a residency filter can *mechanically* improve `useful/attempted` by moving absent keys out of the denominator, which would confuse selection quality with bookkeeping choice.

V2.1 enforces a terminal partition. Every considered key lands in exactly one class:

$$
\text{considered} = \text{primary\_redundant} + \text{secondary\_absent} + \text{gate\_reject} + \text{capacity\_skip} + \text{submitted} + \text{shadow\_submit} + \text{lookup\_unresolved} + \text{cancelled}
$$

The last two are additions to the reviewed design. `cancelled` exists because a bundle can be killed by request completion, preemption, or cache reset *after* its residency resolved; folding those into `gate_reject` would make the gate-rejection rate a function of request-finish timing and corrupt the very signal V2.0 calibration depends on.

The invariant is structural rather than conventional. There is one write point, and it increments `considered` and exactly one terminal counter together:

```python
def _finalize_keys(self, terminal_counter: str, count: int) -> None:
    if count <= 0:
        return
    self._stats.increase_counter(CONSIDERED, count, labelvalues=self._tier_label)
    self._stats.increase_counter(terminal_counter, count, labelvalues=self._tier_label)
```

State transitions are counted separately from terminal classes, so async retries can never double-count. The V1 counters (`prefetch_useful`, `prefetch_late`, `prefetch_load_failed`, …) continue to advance on submission, so V1 and V2 cells remain directly comparable on the same denominators.

The policy is pluggable by design — `PrefetchPolicyFactory` mirrors the existing `CachePolicyFactory` idiom including the out-of-tree `policy_module_path` escape hatch. That is the shape an upstream RFC would propose: a prefetch heuristic as an interchangeable policy object, not a fork of the manager.

### Per-step control flow

The policy hook sits inside `TieringOffloadingManager.on_schedule_end`, at a specific point:

```
1. _maybe_process_finished_jobs()     completions visible, cost samples recorded
2. tier.serve_external_requests()     demand gets its turn first
3. reset per-step gate
4. policy.step(context)               ◄── HERE
5. _flush_pending_promotions()        submissions join this step's batch
6. tier.on_schedule_end()             async lookups flushed to the worker
```

After (1) so completed promotions are visible; after (2) so demand has first claim on capacity; before (5) so this step's submissions ride the same batched `submit_load`; before (6) so lookups issued in `step()` are flushed this step and can resolve next step.

Within `step()`, bundles are driven **earliest-deadline-first**. Admission order would hand the per-step budget to the requests closest to running — the ones with the least lead time and therefore the least to gain — and make the bundles that could still be hidden wait behind them.

## 10. What the code review changed

A `/thermo-nuclear-code-quality-review` of the branch, backed by a 60-seed randomized stress harness against the real manager, produced three findings. All are fixed on the branch.

**Budget contention was discarding resolved work, with priority inversion.** A bundle that did not fit the remaining per-step budget was terminalized as `capacity_skip` and never retried. Since the tier flushes its lookup batch once per step, bundles resolve in *bursts*, so this hit hard. Measured on a 20-request burst: 64 of 200 keys submitted, 126 permanently discarded, and a second step recovered nothing. The fix separates two things that were conflated — a **hard cap** (larger than a whole step's budget; no later step could fit it either, so it stays terminal) from **step contention** (temporary; the next step restores the budget, so defer). After: 190 of 200 submitted, zero discarded. The remaining 10 belong to a request admitted to an empty queue, correctly declining for zero lead time.

**A promotion could report success while contributing no keys to the job.** `_initiate_promotion` returned `True` whenever allocation returned non-`None`, including when `keys_to_store` was empty — which would strand the policy waiting on a completion that could never arrive, hanging the bundle in `SUBMITTED` forever. Unreachable today behind two independent guards, but the policy was trusting a contract the manager never stated. Now returns `False`.

**A missing `exclude_tier` guard.** `on_new_request` gated the policy hook on `exclude_tier is None`; `on_request_finished` did not. The p2p tier calls `parent.on_request_finished(ctx)` with its own serve context, which could therefore cancel a scheduler request's bundle. Benign only because p2p namespaces those IDs — correctness resting on a naming convention in another module. Guarded.

The review also verified, and found sound: the speculative eviction accounting, the demand/speculative race (both `lookup` and `prepare_load` mark demanded before anything pins a block), shared-key collisions across requests (the `ref_cnt=-1` → `HIT_PENDING` dedup prevents owner overwrite), deadline expiry and engine quiescence, and `reset_cache` mid-flight.

Performance was measured rather than asserted. The first EDF implementation recomputed the sort key per bundle per step and cost +40% p50 / +120% p99 in the worst case. Storing the deadline as a fixed attribute and sorting with `attrgetter` brought it back within noise of the original. At the default 256-bundle cap, `step()` is p50 0.019 ms / p99 0.144 ms; at 1024 bundles it reaches p99 0.57 ms, over half the sub-millisecond budget, which is worth knowing before raising that knob.

## 11. Observability

Shadow mode's entire output is metrics, so instrumentation is not decoration here. 20 counters, 5 gauges, 7 histograms. What each answers:

| Question | Metric |
|---|---|
| Where did every candidate key go? | the eight terminal counters; they sum to `considered` |
| Are the keys even on NVMe? | `..._secondary_absent` / `..._considered` |
| Is there contiguous reusable prefix? | `..._bundle_chunks` histogram |
| Is lead time the binding constraint? | `..._deadline_margin_seconds`, as a `le="0"` / `+Inf` share |
| What is the gate actually spending? | `..._transfer_cost_per_chunk_seconds` gauge |
| Is the tier contended? | the same gauge, rising over time |
| Is the lead-time estimator right? | `..._lead_time_seconds` vs `..._actual_lead_time_seconds` |
| What did bundles end up as? | `..._bundle_outcomes` by outcome label |

The margin histogram has negative buckets (negative means the deadline rejected it), so it is consumed as a ratio rather than a quantile — `histogram_quantile` behaves poorly across zero.

Known gaps: GPU **core** utilization is unavailable in this environment (KV occupancy is not a substitute, and every ABC report has had to say so), and there is no per-tier in-flight job depth, so storage queueing is inferred from the cost model rather than measured.

## 12. How to run it

One image serves every cell; selection is entirely runtime config.

```json
"kv_connector_extra_config": {
  "spec_name": "TieringOffloadingSpec",
  "cpu_bytes_to_use": 274877906944,
  "secondary_tiers": [{"type": "fs", "root_dir": "/mnt/nvme-kv-cache",
                       "n_read_threads": 64, "n_write_threads": 64}],
  "prefetch": {"enabled": true, "shadow_mode": true}
}
```

| Cell | kv-config | Image |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | reactive (neither knob) | stock `v0.27.0` |
| 2 | reactive | `v0.27.0-prefetch-v2` |
| 3 | `prefetch.shadow_mode: true` | `v0.27.0-prefetch-v2` |
| 4 | `admission_prefetch_chunks: 100` | `v0.27.0-prefetch-v2` |
| 5 | `prefetch.shadow_mode: false` | `v0.27.0-prefetch-v2` |

`admission_prefetch_chunks > 0` and `prefetch.enabled` are mutually exclusive and rejected together at startup. Both paths require the per-request opt-in `kv_transfer_params.abc_admission_prefetch: true`, which the AgentX profile already sends; the stock image ignores the unknown key, so cell 1 needs no profile change.

Cells 1 and 2 are a **regression gate** — same config, different image, and they must match, because the V2 changes touch the demand path (eviction victim selection, demand marking). Cell 3 is a **decision gate**: shadow mode moves no data, so if `secondary_absent` dominates there, the keys are not on NVMe and the live cell cannot help.

BenchFlow config lives in `experiments/rhoai/abc.yaml`; the 21 V2 PromQL queries were added to `profiles/metrics/detailed.yaml`, which is an allowlist — without them a shadow run collects nothing.

**Shadow mode defaults to `true`.** Live submission is an explicit opt-in.

## 13. What we expect to see

A falsifiable prediction, stated before the run. From the C64 numbers — mean waiting 6.22 requests at 0.676 req/s — Little's law gives a mean queue wait of

$$W = \frac{L}{\lambda} = \frac{6.22}{0.676} \approx 9.2\ \text{s}$$

against a 100-chunk bundle transfer of roughly $100 \times 2.2\,\text{ms} \approx 220\,\text{ms}$. The available window is about **40× larger than the transfer needs**.

If that holds, `deadline_missed_share` should be near zero and `gate_reject` minimal — meaning **residency, not timing, is what V2.1 actually fixes**, and V1's 87% load-fail rate was the real ceiling. If instead the margin comes back negative, either the lead-time estimator or the cost model is wrong, and the predicted-vs-actual lead-time histogram pair identifies which.

## 14. Related work

The design sits in a crowded but not identical space.

**TokenCake** ([arXiv:2510.18586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18586)) and **KVFlow** ([arXiv:2507.07400](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07400)) both exploit agent workflow structure for KV management — TokenCake co-optimizing scheduling and memory around function-call boundaries, KVFlow using workflow-derived reuse distance. Both support the premise that orchestration state beats reactive LRU. Neither addresses the residency-verification problem that dominated V1's failures, because both assume the data is where you think it is.

**PEEK** ([arXiv:2607.02525](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02525)) is the closest in spirit: proactive KV movement driven by queue-informed prediction. The distinction here is the *gate* — V2.1 will decline to move data when the predicted window cannot hide the measured transfer, rather than moving optimistically and recovering.

**AgentKVShift** ([arXiv:2607.21604](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21604)) and **RelayCaching** ([arXiv:2603.13289](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13289)) address semantic reuse and drift across agent contexts — reusing KV that is *nearly* right. Orthogonal to this work and parked as future RFC material.

**Average Eviction Time** ([USENIX ATC '16](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc16/technical-sessions/presentation/hu)) was in the original V2 design as a per-key eviction countdown. The theoretical validation correctly rejected that reading: AET derives *population-level* eviction time from reuse-time distributions and does not give a cheap per-key time-to-eviction. It is retained only as a candidate global pressure signal.

**llm-d precise prefix-cache-aware routing** already does exact, event-driven per-pod KV residency indexing combined with load. This bounds the novelty claim honestly: cache awareness is not new, and any routing-level claim must beat that baseline. The defensible delta is narrower — *predicted future reuse and deadlines derived from queue state*, used to decide movement rather than placement.

Within vLLM, **PagedAttention** gives the block-level granularity this operates on, and **LMCache**'s chunk grouping is the reason a 16-token block is not the transfer unit. The upstream RFC target is vLLM first, following the shape of the Mooncake connector RFC ([vllm-project/vllm#38474](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/38474)).

## 15. What is deliberately not done

- **No learned model, anywhere.** Removed from the program, not deferred. The predictors that matter are structural.
- **Secondary→CPU only.** No Hot/Warm/Cool/Cold placement, no GPU admission decisions. Those are separate control surfaces with separate costs.
- **No lifecycle events.** Tool-call and handoff signals need an out-of-band, session-addressed control API; request-scoped `kv_transfer_params` cannot arrive early enough. That is V2.2.
- **Single tier index.** Multi-tier candidacy is out of scope.
- **Preempted-then-resumed requests are not re-admitted.** They fall back to the demand path.
- **Four bounds are still configured, not derived.** `max_pending_bundles`, `max_promotions_per_step`, and `max_candidate_chunks` could come from `max_num_seqs` and `max_model_len`, which the connector already receives. `speculative_max_bytes` is arguably redundant now that allocation is non-evicting.

The config surface went from 17 knobs to 13, of which two are on/off switches and only one — the EWMA smoothing factor — is a genuinely dimensionless tuning parameter. The rest are bounds or wiring.

## 16. Status and next step

Implemented, tested, shadow-default, **not yet benchmarked**. No performance claim is made or possible from this document.

The next action is the five-cell sequence in §12, starting with the 1↔2 regression gate and reading cell 3 before committing time to 4 and 5. Cell 3 doubles as the V2.0 characterization the phased plan calls for: it measures the lead-time distribution, reusable prefix sizes, secondary residency, and — through the self-calibrating cost model fed by demand traffic — the transfer curve, all without moving a speculative byte.

One caveat for the write-up: cell 4 is not numerically the V1 whose numbers are in the KB. Under this branch, V1's completed promotions are marked speculative and reclaimed before demand blocks, and the untracked accounting changed. It is the right baseline for isolating what V2's selection and gating add, but quote cell 4's own numbers rather than the published C64 figures.
